Political analytics · California · October 7, 2003

How did Arnold Schwarzenegger become governor?

A data-first look at the recall of Gray Davis. It sets out the evidence for each competing explanation, says how strong that evidence is, and avoids judging whether the outcome was good or bad.

Question 1 · recall Davis?
Yes (remove)55.4%

4,976,274 yes vs 4,007,783 no; 9,413,494 Californians voted in the election (joincalifornia.com, official tallies). The ballot had two parts: first whether to recall Davis, then who should replace him. A plurality was enough on the second question, and 135 candidates were on it. No runoff was held.

Two things follow from the numbers alone. (a) Schwarzenegger won with a plurality, not a majority, but he had a large lead. (b) The two Republican candidates together took about 62% of replacement votes, while Republicans were about 35% of registered voters at the time (CNN). Turnout differences and crossover voting could both explain that gap, and this data alone can’t say how much comes from each.

2. Explanation A: Davis was deeply unpopular

Strong evidence

Exit polling reported about 7 in 10 voters disapproving of Davis’s job performance, with nearly half disapproving strongly. Among that strong-disapprove group, roughly 9 in 10 voted to recall and 7 in 10 voted Schwarzenegger. Contemporary coverage points to the 2000–01 energy crisis and a record deficit estimated at $38 billion, which led to a tripled vehicle tax and 30%-plus fee increases at public universities.

Limits: approval data shows who was unhappy, not why each person voted. Whether Davis was responsible for those conditions is disputed and is not something this data can settle.

Polls varied by pollster and voter screen, so this is not a clean trend. The recall was likely to pass, but the margin was uncertain. Polls consistently showed Schwarzenegger leading the replacement field once he entered on August 6–7.

5. Explanation D: candidate profile

Moderate evidence

A pre-election Cal State Fullerton survey of Orange County (a Republican-leaning area, so not representative of the state) found 65% supporting him in some way and 31% of surveyed Democrats naming him as a preferred replacement. Exit-poll coverage (Christian Science Monitor) reported that anti-abortion voters preferred Schwarzenegger, who supports abortion rights, over McClintock, who opposes it. Both are consistent with a broad coalition of moderates, independents and some Democrats, rather than a strictly partisan one.

Limits: celebrity name recognition, his moderate positions and his “outsider” image are hard to separate with this data. A survey is not a vote.

6. Explanation E: structure and the Democratic response

Plausible, harder to test

Voters could vote on the replacement even without voting on the recall, and 135 candidates were on the ballot. Davis’s supporters argued in later years that the presence of a prominent Democrat, Lt. Gov. Cruz Bustamante, on the replacement ballot gave Democrats a backup option and may have made voting “yes” easier. Bustamante finished a distant second. The data cannot show whether a unified Democratic “No on recall, no replacement” strategy would have changed the outcome.

7. Explanation F: it was an organized partisan effort

Contested

Davis and recall opponents framed the recall as an attempt by Republicans to take the governorship after a statewide loss in 2002. Coverage noted that Rep. Darrell Issa financed the petition effort. Recall supporters countered that the anger was bipartisan, and exit polls did show about a quarter of Democrats voting yes. Both claims have some basis in the figures above: the signature drive had partisan funding, and the vote itself included substantial crossover.

What the evidence supports, and what it doesn’t

Best supported: the recall passed because of widespread disapproval of Davis (A), including defections from groups he previously relied on (B). Schwarzenegger then won the replacement question by drawing a mix of Republicans, independents and some Democrats (D).

Plausible but not settled: how much the ballot rules and Democratic strategy mattered (E), and how much the recall was a partisan project (F).

Not answerable here: whether Davis deserved blame for the energy crisis or the deficit, whether Schwarzenegger was the better choice, and what a different ballot design would have produced.
